Adverbs: Types and Position

Place the adverb where the sentence expects it.

🎯 What you can communicate after this page: You can place adverbs of manner, frequency, time and comment in the right slot.
Examples in context
  • She rarely misses a deadline. Frequency before the main verb. — Mid-position.
  • He spoke quietly and left. Manner after the verb. — End position.
  • Fortunately, the audit found nothing. Comment on the whole clause. — Front position.

👀 What do you observe?

Adverbs fall into types that prefer different positions. Manner adverbs go at the end (she spoke quietly) or after the object. Frequency adverbs take mid-position, before the main verb and after the auxiliary (she rarely misses, she has rarely missed). Time adverbs go at the end or the front. Comment adverbs, which judge the whole sentence, take the front position with a comma (fortunately, admittedly).

manner end, after the verb or object he spoke quietly
frequency mid-position she rarely misses a deadline
time end or front we met yesterday / yesterday we met
comment front with a comma fortunately, the audit found nothing
degree before the adjective or adverb the results were surprisingly strong
linking adverb front with a comma however, the data was incomplete

Formula / rule: Decide the type first, then the position: manner at the end, frequency in the middle, time at either end, comment at the front with a comma. Never put an adverb between the verb and its object: she speaks English well, not she speaks well English.

★ Why it matters

Adverb position is where word order carries meaning: only rarely does the difference between often and rarely depend on where they sit. A misplaced frequency adverb also changes emphasis, which is why he rarely is late and he is rarely late are both possible but not equivalent in rhythm.

💭 Think like a native speaker

Native speakers vary the position for emphasis: mid-position is neutral, front position emphasises, and end position is the default in speech. In academic writing, front-position comment adverbs are frequent and mid-position hedges are frequent.

🗣️ Pronunciation note

The adverb carries the stress when it is fronted: FORTUNATELY, the audit found nothing. In mid-position it is usually unstressed.

Additional example bank
  • Manner after the object: She answered the question calmly.
  • Frequency mid-position: The board has never rejected a proposal.
  • Comment: Admittedly, the timetable was optimistic.
⚠️ Common mistakes to avoid

❌ Wrong: She speaks well English (adverb between the verb and its object)

✅ Right: She speaks English well

❌ Wrong: Rarely she misses a deadline (frequency adverb before the subject, no inversion)

✅ Right: She rarely misses a deadline
