Narrative Tenses

Tell a story with the tenses that order it.

🎯 What you can communicate after this page: You can move between past simple, past continuous, past perfect and used to when telling a story.
Examples in context
  • The rain was falling and the streets were empty. Background, past continuous. — Sets the scene.
  • Then the lights went out. Event, past simple. — Moves the story.
  • Someone had cut the cable. Earlier event, past perfect. — Explains what we found.

👀 What do you observe?

A narrative uses three layers. Past continuous for the scene and the action in progress; past simple for the events that move the story forward, in order; past perfect for what happened before the moment being told. Used to and would mark repeated past behaviour, and the past perfect continuous marks a state that had been going on before an event.

Element / structure Role in English Application example
past continuous scene and action in progress we were waiting when the call came
past simple events in sequence he walked in, sat down and said nothing
past perfect earlier event, explains the scene someone had cut the cable
past perfect continuous state continuing before an event they had been arguing for an hour
used to / would repeated past behaviour they used to meet on Fridays
when / while interruption or simultaneity while we were waiting, the line failed

Formula / rule: Set the scene in the past continuous, tell the events in the past simple in order, and reach back with the past perfect when the reader needs to know what came before. Use when for an interruption after a continuous clause, and while for two simultaneous actions.

★ Why it matters

Narrative tense control is what makes a story readable: a reader who cannot tell which event happened first stops following. Past perfect is the tool that lets a writer explain a consequence before revealing the cause, which is the basic move of report writing as well as fiction.

💭 Think like a native speaker

Native speakers use the past perfect sparingly in speech and rely on before, after and then to order events; overusing it in writing sounds literary. Would for past habit is common in storytelling (we would sit for hours) but not for single events.

🗣️ Pronunciation note

The past perfect is usually contracted in speech (she'd already left), which is why the auxiliary carries the stress when the order is the point: she HAD already left.

Additional example bank
  • Scene: The office was emptying and the phones had stopped ringing.
  • Sequence: He opened the file, read the first page and closed it again.
  • Earlier: By the time the auditors arrived, the records had disappeared.
⚠️ Common mistakes to avoid

❌ Wrong: When I arrived, she already left (past simple for an earlier event)

✅ Right: When I arrived, she had already left

❌ Wrong: While we waited, the line failed (past simple where an action in progress is meant)

✅ Right: While we were waiting, the line failed
