Level B2 Grammar

Speculation and Deduction

Say how certain you are about the past and the present.

🎯 What you can communicate after this page: You can use must, may, might and can't to reason about what happened.
Examples in context
  • Certain, past: The file must have been deleted. — The only explanation.
  • Possible, past: She may have missed the train. — One of several.
  • Impossible, past: He can't have known. — Logically excluded.
  • Expected, past: They should have finished by now. — Expected but not confirmed.

👀 What do you observe?

Deduction uses modals in a graded scale. Certainty: must (affirmative) and can't (negative). Possibility: may, might, could. Expectation: should, ought to. For the past, the modal is followed by have + past participle: must have left, might have left, can't have left, should have left. For the present, the modal takes a plain infinitive: must be, might be, can't be.

Element / structure Role in English Application example
must have + participle logical certainty about the past the file must have been deleted
can't / couldn't have + participle impossibility about the past he can't have known
may / might / could have + participle possibility about the past she may have missed the train
must be / can't be certainty about the present they must be stuck
should have + participle expectation, often unmet they should have arrived by now
seem / appear to have reported impression the plan appears to have failed

Formula / rule: Argue from the evidence to the modal: if the evidence leaves only one explanation, use must; if it excludes one, use can't; if several remain, use may, might or could; if you simply expected it, use should. Then set the time: plain infinitive for now, have + participle for before now.

★ Why it matters

This is the grammar of inference, and it is what makes a report read as reasoning rather than assertion. Must have signals that the writer has weighed the alternatives; might have keeps the file open; can't have closes it. Used well, the scale lets a writer commit exactly as far as the evidence allows.

💭 Think like a native speaker

In speech, must have reduces to must've and might have to might've, which sound almost identical to must of and might of, a common misspelling. Could have is possibility, not ability, in this use, and can't have is far more common than couldn't have in British English.

🗣️ Pronunciation note

The modal carries the stress when the deduction is the point: they MUST have left. In must've the auxiliary disappears and the participle carries the stress.

Additional example bank
  • Present certainty: The office must be empty; the lights are off.
  • Past possibility: The message could have been lost in the queue.
  • Expectation: The auditors should have signed off by Friday.
⚠️ Common mistakes to avoid

❌ Wrong: They must left early (modal with past simple instead of have + participle)

✅ Right: They must have left early

❌ Wrong: He can't knew about it (can't with past simple)

✅ Right: He can't have known about it
