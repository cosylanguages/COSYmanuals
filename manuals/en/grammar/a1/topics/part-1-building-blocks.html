Unit 1: The English Alphabet & Mechanics

1.1 Capital Letters vs. Small Letters

Rules: Always capitalize the pronoun I, proper names (David, London), days of the week (Monday), months (May), nationalities (British), and the first word of a sentence.

Example:

My name is John. I live in London and I speak English.

1.2 Alphabetical Order & Dictionary Skills

Ordering words alphabetically relies on comparing letters from left to right: first letter (apple, banana, cat), second letter (cat, coat, cup), or third letter (straight, street, string).

1.3 Spelling Names & Addresses

Dictation phrases for spelling names, emails, and addresses aloud:

How do you spell your name? / Can you spell that, please? / Capital A, double L...

A: How do you spell your last name?
B: S-M-I-T-H.

1.4 Common Abbreviations

Everyday short forms and abbreviations used in written and spoken English:

  • ID: Identity Document
  • TV: Television
  • VIP: Very Important Person
  • e.g.: exempli gratia (for example)
  • i.e.: id est (that is / in other words)
  • PC: Personal Computer
  • UK: United Kingdom
  • US: United States

Unit 2: Sounds & Pronunciation Mechanics

2.1 Vowels vs. Consonants

The English alphabet contains 5 vowel letters (A, E, I, O, U) representing 20 distinct vowel sounds and 21 consonant letters (B, C, D, F, G, H, J, K, L, M, N, P, Q, R, S, T, V, W, X, Y, Z).

2.2 Short vs. Long Vowel Pairs & The Magic-E Rule

Vowel length changes word meaning completely:

  • /ɪ/ vs /iː/ (ship / sheep, sit / seat, fit / feet)
  • /æ/ vs /ɑː/ (cat / car, hat / heart)

The Silent-E / Magic-E Rule: A single silent e at the end of a word turns a short vowel into a long vowel that "says its name":

  • kit /kɪt/ → kite /kaɪt/
  • hop /hɒp/ → hope /həʊp/
  • cut /kʌt/ → cute /kjuːt/

2.3 Common Consonant Digraphs

Two consonant letters written together that make a single new sound:

  • SH /ʃ/: shop, shoe, fish
  • CH /ʧ/: chair, cheese, watch
  • PH /f/: phone, photo, elephant
  • WH /w/ or /h/: white, wheel, who
  • NG /ŋ/: sing, long, English

2.5 Silent Letters in Common Words

Letters that are written but not pronounced:

  • Silent K: know, knee, knife
  • Silent L: listen, talk, walk, half
  • Silent W: write, answer, wrong
  • Silent B: climb, comb

2.6 Word Stress in Nouns & Occupations

In English, most two-syllable nouns and occupations have first-syllable stress:

DOC-tor, TEA-cher, WA-ter, BU-sin-ess

2.7 Sentence Stress & Schwa Sound /ə/

Content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives) are stressed; function words (prepositions, articles, auxiliaries) are unstressed. Unstressed syllables often use the schwa sound /ə/:

about /əˈbaʊt/, doctor /ˈdɒk.tə/, teacher /ˈtiː.tʃə/

2.8 Regular Endings: -ed and -s Pronunciation

Regular past tense -ed has three distinct sounds: /ɪd/ after /t/ or /d/ (wanted, needed), /t/ after unvoiced sounds (worked, stopped), and /d/ after voiced sounds (played, lived).

Plural and 3rd person singular -s / -es endings are pronounced /ɪz/ after sibilants (buses, watches), /s/ after unvoiced sounds (cats, books), and /z/ after voiced sounds (dogs, runs).

2.9 Basic Intonation

  • Rising intonation (↗) in Yes/No questions: Are you ready? ↗
  • Falling intonation (↘) in Wh- questions & statements: Where do you live? ↘ / I live in London. ↘

2.10 Connected Speech & Contractions

In natural speech, words flow together in continuous speech streams. Final consonants link smoothly to initial vowels (an apple ➔ /ənæp.əl/, stand up ➔ /stændʌp/). Unstressed function words reduce to weak schwa forms (fish and chips ➔ /fɪʃ ən ʧɪps/), and contractions combine pronouns and auxiliary verbs seamlessly (it's, I'm, don't, can't).

2.11 R-Controlled Vowels

When a vowel is followed by R, the R controls and alters the vowel sound:

  • AR /ɑː/: car, park, star
  • OR /ɔː/: for, born, short
  • ER, IR, UR /ɜː/: her, bird, turn
  • AIR /eə/ & EAR /ɪə/: fair, near

2.12 Suffix Sound Shifts (-tion, -sion, -cian)

Special suffix endings shift their pronunciation to /ʃən/ or /ʒən/:

  • -tion & -cian /ʃən/: nation /ˈneɪʃən/, musician /mjuːˈzɪʃən/
  • -sion /ʃən/ or /ʒən/: discussion /dɪˈskʌʃən/ (after consonant) vs. vision /ˈvɪʒən/ (after vowel)

Unit 3: Numbers, Money, Dates & Time

3.1 Cardinal Numbers 0–1000 & Stress Rules

Numbers 0–20: zero, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty.

Stress distinction:

  • -teen numbers: stress the second syllable (thir-TEEN, four-TEEN)
  • -ty numbers: stress the first syllable (THIR-ty, FOUR-ty)

Tens & Big Numbers: thirty, forty, fifty, sixty, seventy, eighty, ninety, one hundred, one thousand, one million.

3.2 Ordinal Numbers & Birthdates

Ordinals indicate order or position: 1st (first), 2nd (second), 3rd (third), 4th (fourth), 21st (twenty-first).

Writing dates: The 15th of May 1998 or May 15th, 1998.

3.3 Telephone Numbers, Room Numbers & Email Dictation

Phone numbers: Read digit by digit; 0 is pronounced "oh" or "zero"; repeated numbers use "double" (07700 double nine...).

Email dictation symbols: @ = at, . = dot, - = hyphen/dash, _ = underscore.

3.4 Money, Prices & Currencies

Major currency symbols:

  • $ = Dollar
  • £ = Pound
  • € = Euro

Reading prices aloud:

  • $5.50 = five dollars fifty or five fifty
  • £12.99 = twelve pounds ninety-nine
  • €8.40 = eight euros forty

3.5 Telling the Time

Digital: 7:15 (seven fifteen), 8:30 (eight thirty).

Analog: 7:15 (quarter past seven), 8:30 (half past eight), 8:50 (ten to nine).

3.6 Basic Fractions & Percentages

Fractions: 1/2 = a half, 1/3 = a third, 1/4 = a quarter.

Percentages: 50% = fifty percent, 100% = one hundred percent.

🗣️ Pronunciation Note

Focus on natural sentence rhythm: emphasize the main content words when expressing The Building Blocks of English, while keeping grammatical function words unstressed.
