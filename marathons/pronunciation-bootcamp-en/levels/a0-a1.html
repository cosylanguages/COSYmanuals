Unit 1 — The English Alphabet & Sound System

14-Field Fiche — Lesson 1.1: Letter Names vs Letter Sounds

Definition
The English alphabet consists of 26 letters (5 vowels, 21 consonants) producing 44 distinct sounds.
Rule
Letter names (e.g. A = /eɪ/) are used for spelling aloud; letter sounds (e.g. A = /æ/ in cat) are used for reading words.
Why?
English orthography retains historical spellings while spoken sounds shifted over centuries.
How to recognize?
Single letters in CVC (consonant-vowel-consonant) patterns usually take their short letter sound.
Common examples
cat /kæt/, dog /dɒɡ/, pen /pɛn/, bus /bʌs/, sit /sɪt/
Counter-examples
Vowel digraphs like EA where two letters represent a single long sound (e.g. tea /tiː/).
Exceptions
Y functions as a consonant at start (/j/ in yes) and a vowel at end (/iː/ in happy).
Common mistakes
Reading CVC words using letter names instead of letter sounds (saying /siː-eɪ-tiː/ for cat).
Mnemonic Hint
Letter NAME is how you spell it; letter SOUND is how you read it!
Key vocabulary

Unit 2 — The Magic E Rule (Silent E)

14-Field Fiche — Lesson 2.1: Long Vowels via Silent E

Definition
A silent E at the end of a single-syllable word changes the preceding vowel from a short sound to its long letter name.
Rule
Pattern V + C + E: Vowel becomes long, E remains silent.
Why?
Historical scribes added final E to indicate open syllable vowel length.
How to recognize?
Look for final -e preceded by one consonant and one vowel (e.g. make, bite, note, cute).
Common examples
hat ➔ hate, bit ➔ bite, hop ➔ hope, cut ➔ cute, pet ➔ Pete
Counter-examples
Words with double consonants before E (e.g. apple, little).
Exceptions
High-frequency function words like give, have, come, love, done.
Common mistakes
Pronouncing the final E sound (e.g. saying /meɪkə/ for make).
Mnemonic Hint
Magic E is silent, but it makes the vowel say its own name!
